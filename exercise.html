Revise, Reflect, Refine — Exercise Questions

Q1. Differentiate between the following pairs of terms based on the clues given in parentheses: (i) Cell membrane and cell wall (permeability) (ii) RER and SER (structure) (iii) Chloroplasts and chromoplasts (pigments)

(i) Cell membrane vs cell wall — permeability. The cell membrane is selectively permeable: it allows only certain substances (water, gases, selected nutrients) to pass and blocks others, so it controls what enters and leaves the cell. The cell wall is fully permeable: water and dissolved minerals pass through it freely with no selection. It gives structural support rather than acting as a control barrier.

(ii) RER vs SER — structure. Rough ER has ribosomes attached to its surface, giving it a rough, bumpy look under the electron microscope; these ribosomes make proteins, so RER is involved in protein synthesis and secretion. Smooth ER has no ribosomes, so it looks smooth; it synthesises and stores fats (lipids) and hormones.

(iii) Chloroplasts vs chromoplasts — pigments. Chloroplasts contain the green pigment chlorophyll, which absorbs sunlight for photosynthesis; they are found in leaves and other green parts. Chromoplasts contain yellow, orange or red pigments (other than chlorophyll); they are found in petals and fruits and give bright colours that attract pollinators and seed-dispersing animals.

Q2. Two similar animal cells are placed in two different solutions: Cell X in pure water, Cell Y in a concentrated salt solution. Cell X swells and Cell Y shrinks. Which statement explains this correctly?
(i) Salt molecules moved into Cell Y, causing it to shrink. (ii) Water moved into Cell X and more water moved out of Cell Y than the salt solution entered in it. (iii) Water moved into Cell X and moved out of Cell Y through the cell membrane. (iv) Solute movement caused osmosis in both cells.

Answer: (iii) — Water moved into Cell X and moved out of Cell Y through the cell membrane.

Explanation. Pure water is hypotonic to Cell X (more solute inside than outside), so water enters the cell by osmosis → Cell X swells. The concentrated salt solution is hypertonic to Cell Y (more solute outside), so water leaves the cell by osmosis → Cell Y shrinks. The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets water through but not salt. Option (i) is wrong because salt does not move in through the membrane; (ii) wrongly says salt solution enters; (iv) is wrong because osmosis is the movement of water, not solute.

Q4. Which option(s) correctly place the pair of cell organelles under the given categories (Present in plant cells / Absent in animal cells)?
(i) Leucoplast / Cell wall (ii) Mitochondria / Ribosome (iii) Cell wall / Golgi apparatus (iv) Lysosome / Endoplasmic reticulum

Answer: (i) — Leucoplast (present in plant cells) and cell wall (absent in animal cells).

Explanation. Leucoplasts are plastids found only in plant cells; the cell wall is present in plant cells and absent in animal cells — so both placements are correct. Option (ii) is wrong because ribosomes are present in animal cells too. Option (iii) is wrong because the Golgi apparatus is present in animal cells. Option (iv) is wrong because both lysosomes and ER are present in animal cells.

Q5. Renu says all parts of a plant, even roots, contain plastids. Rohit says plastids are absent in roots because roots are underground and do not photosynthesise. Who is correct? Justify.

Renu is correct; Rohit is only partly right.

Rohit is right that roots are underground, receive no light and do not perform photosynthesis, so chloroplasts are absent in root cells. But plastids are of three kinds — chloroplasts, chromoplasts and leucoplasts. Roots contain leucoplasts, colourless plastids that store food such as starch, oils and proteins. Leucoplasts do not need light because their job is storage, not food-making. Potato tubers and taro (Colocasia) are well-known examples where leucoplasts store large amounts of starch. Some roots (e.g., carrot) even contain chromoplasts. So plastids are present in all parts of a plant, including roots — just not the same type everywhere.

Q6. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are two important organelles in a plant cell. Discuss how they are structurally and functionally similar to and different from each other.

Similarities

  • Both are double-membrane-bound organelles.
  • Both contain their own DNA and their own ribosomes, so they can make some of their own proteins.
  • Both have a semi-fluid interior (matrix in mitochondria, stroma in chloroplasts).
  • Both are involved in energy transformations in the cell.
  • Both resemble bacteria, suggesting a shared evolutionary history with single-celled organisms.

Differences

In short: mitochondria "burn" food to release energy; chloroplasts use sunlight to make food.

Q7. Which of the following pairs of cell organelles contains DNA?
(i) Chloroplasts, Ribosomes (ii) Mitochondria, Nucleus (iii) Golgi bodies, Ribosomes (iv) Nucleus, Lysosomes

Answer: (ii) — Mitochondria and Nucleus.

Explanation. The nucleus contains chromosomes made of DNA. Mitochondria have their own small circular DNA (like bacteria). Chloroplasts also have DNA, but they are paired with ribosomes in option (i), and ribosomes contain RNA and protein, not DNA. Golgi bodies and lysosomes contain no DNA.

Q8. A researcher placed one carrot in plain water and another similar carrot in concentrated salt solution for 24 hours (Fig. 2.21). (i) What hypothesis does she want to test? (ii) How could the experiment be improved? (iii) Why does the carrot in plain water stay stiff and crunchy while the one in salt solution becomes rubbery and limp?

(i) Hypothesis: that water moves into or out of plant (carrot) cells by osmosis depending on the concentration of the surrounding solution — i.e., a difference in solute concentration between the inside of the cells and the outside solution causes water movement across the cell membrane and changes the firmness of the carrot.

(ii) Improvements: weigh (and measure) both carrots before and after to get a measurable change in mass; use equal-sized pieces cut from the same carrot for a fair comparison; keep other conditions the same — same volume of liquid, same temperature, same duration; repeat the experiment several times; try a range of salt concentrations to find the point where no change occurs (isotonic).

(iii) Explanation: Plain water is hypotonic to the carrot cells — water enters the cells by osmosis; the vacuoles fill, the contents press against the cell walls, the cells become turgid, and the carrot stays stiff and crunchy. The concentrated salt solution is hypertonic — water leaves the cells by osmosis; the cells lose pressure and become flaccid, so the carrot turns rubbery and limp.

Q10. Potato cup experiment: four peeled potato cups in beakers of water — Cup A empty, Cup B with sugar, Cup C with salt, Cup D (made from a boiled potato) with sugar. After two hours: (i) Why does water gather in Cups B and C? (ii) Why is Cup A necessary? (iii) Why does water not gather in Cups A and D?

(i) The sugar in Cup B and the salt in Cup C dissolve in the little moisture there and make the inside of the cup a hypertonic (concentrated) region compared with the plain water in the beaker. The potato wall is made of living cells with selectively permeable membranes. Water therefore moves by osmosis from the beaker (dilute) through the potato cells into the hollow (concentrated), and collects there.

(ii) Cup A is the control. It has no solute, so no osmosis should occur. If water did collect in Cup A, we would know the water in B and C was due to something else (leakage, for example). Cup A proves that the water gathering in B and C is caused specifically by the solute concentration difference.

(iii) Cup A: there is no concentration difference between inside and outside, so there is no net osmosis and no water gathers. Cup D: boiling kills the potato cells and destroys their membranes. A dead membrane is no longer selectively permeable, so osmosis cannot take place even though sugar is present — no water gathers. This shows that osmosis needs a living, selectively permeable membrane.

Q11. Identify the pair that incorrectly matches the cell organelle with its function: (i) Ribosome — protein synthesis (ii) SER — lipid and cellulose synthesis (iii) Lysosome — digestion of foreign agents

Answer: (ii) — SER: lipid and cellulose synthesis is the incorrect match.

Explanation. (i) is correct — ribosomes make proteins. (iii) is correct — lysosomes contain enzymes that digest foreign agents and worn-out parts. (ii) is wrong because the smooth ER synthesises lipids and hormones, not cellulose. Cellulose is the material of the plant cell wall and is not made by the SER.

Q12. What outcome do you expect if all the mitochondria are removed from a eukaryotic cell?

The cell would lose its main source of energy and would soon die.

  • Cellular respiration stops — glucose can no longer be broken down with oxygen to release energy.
  • ATP production stops — the cell runs out of its energy currency.
  • All energy-dependent activities halt: protein synthesis, transport of materials across membranes, movement, cell division, communication with other cells.
  • The cell cannot repair itself or reproduce.
  • Only a tiny amount of energy could come from anaerobic breakdown of glucose in the cytoplasm (glycolysis), far too little to sustain normal functions for long.

In short, removing all mitochondria is fatal for a eukaryotic cell, because it depends on them for its energy supply.

Q13. Which phenomenon inhibits the formation of tumours in the human body? Can plants also develop tumours? Explain.

Contact inhibition. In normal animal cells, when a cell comes into contact with neighbouring cells, it receives a signal to stop dividing. This keeps cells in an orderly single layer and prevents overcrowding, so tumours do not form. Cancer cells lose contact inhibition — they ignore the stop signal, keep dividing uncontrollably and pile up into a tumour, which may invade nearby tissues and spread.

Can plants develop tumours? Yes, but differently. Plant cells have rigid cell walls and do not show contact inhibition; they follow a different pattern of growth. Plants can develop tumour-like growths called galls — for example, crown gall caused by the bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens, or growths triggered by insects and abnormal cell division. Because plant cells are fixed by their walls and cannot move, these growths stay localised and do not spread through the plant the way animal cancers spread, so they are generally less dangerous.

Q14. The cell membrane is made of proteins and lipids. Which organelles help in the synthesis of the cell membrane? Write the path of these compounds from their site of synthesis to the cell membrane and show it through a labelled diagram.

Organelles involved: ribosomes, rough ER (RER), smooth ER (SER) and the Golgi apparatus.

Path of proteins:

Ribosomes attached to the RER synthesise the membrane proteins.
The proteins enter the RER, where they are folded and initially processed.
The RER buds off transport vesicles that carry the proteins to the Golgi apparatus.
The Golgi modifies, sorts and packages the proteins into vesicles.
These vesicles travel to the plasma membrane and fuse with it — the vesicle membrane, with its proteins, becomes part of the cell membrane.

Path of lipids:

The SER synthesises lipids (fats).
Lipids move (in vesicles) to the Golgi apparatus and are packaged.
Vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane, adding new lipid molecules to it.

Overall: Ribosome/RER (proteins) and SER (lipids) → Golgi apparatus → vesicles → plasma membrane. (See the animated pathway diagram on Topic 5 — Fig. 2.13.)

Q15. What would happen if gametes were formed by mitotic divisions?

Mitosis produces cells with the full (diploid) number of chromosomes. If sperm and eggs were made by mitosis, each gamete would carry the full set instead of half.

  • At fertilisation the zygote would receive a full set from each parent → double the normal chromosome number.
  • This doubling would repeat every generation; within a few generations the chromosome number would be so large that cells could not function, and the species would not survive.
  • Mitosis produces genetically identical cells, so all gametes — and all offspring — would be identical: no variation.
  • Without variation, populations could not adapt to changing environments, and evolution would stop.

That is why gametes are made by meiosis, which halves the chromosome number and shuffles the chromosomes, so fertilisation restores the normal number and produces unique offspring.

Q16. Farmer Deepa preserved excess amla and lemons as pickles, murabbas and sharbat by adding salt, sugar or jaggery. (i) Which scientific concept has she applied? (ii) How do high concentrations of salt and sugar prevent the growth of spoilage-causing bacteria and fungi? (iii) Suggest a healthy recipe of this kind. (iv) What scientific values are addressed in this case?

(i) She has applied osmosis (movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane) to preserve food.

(ii) High concentrations of salt, sugar or jaggery make the surrounding solution hypertonic compared with the inside of microbial cells. Water therefore moves out of the bacterial and fungal cells by osmosis; the cells lose water, shrink (their contents pull away from the wall), and can no longer grow or multiply. With no active microbes the food does not spoil — which is why pickles, jams and murabbas keep for months.

(iii) Healthy recipe — Amla murabba: wash and prick amla with a fork; blanch briefly in boiling water to soften; prepare a syrup with sugar (or jaggery) roughly equal in weight to the amla; add the amla with a little cardamom and saffron; simmer until the syrup thickens; cool and store in a clean, dry, sterilised glass jar. (Similar: lemon pickle with salt and turmeric; amla sharbat concentrate with jaggery.)

(iv) Values: applying scientific knowledge (osmosis) to real problems; reducing food waste and improving food security; using traditional knowledge in a scientifically sound way; sustainability and economic self-reliance; curiosity, observation and innovation.

Pause and Ponder — in-text questions

P1. What argument would you give for the necessity of a cell wall in plants (usually fixed in one place) versus animals (usually moving from place to place)?

Plants are rooted in one location and cannot move to escape strong winds, heavy rain or temperature extremes. The rigid cell wall gives them the structural strength and protection to remain upright under these stresses. Animals can move to seek shelter or avoid danger, and their cells must be flexible to allow movement, so they do not need — and would be hindered by — a rigid wall.

P2. What consequences would you predict for a plant cell if its cell wall became as flexible as a cell membrane?

The plant would lose its rigidity and ability to stand upright, quickly wilting or collapsing. The cells would also be highly vulnerable to bursting in dilute (hypotonic) environments, because they rely on the rigid wall to push back against the pressure of water entering by osmosis.

P3. Why is it important to cut the two potato pieces in roughly equal size and measure their initial weight before placing them in different liquids?

Equal size ensures both pieces have a similar surface area, so the rate and extent of osmosis are comparable — a fair test. Recording the initial weight gives a baseline; without it, we could not calculate how much water each piece gained or lost.

P4. Do white flowers contain any pigment? Give reasons.

White flowers generally contain no white pigment. Their cells have colourless plastids — leucoplasts — used for storing starch, oils or proteins. The white appearance comes from the reflection and scattering of all visible light by microscopic air spaces and cell structures in the petal tissue.

P6. Instead of many small ones, why does a cell not have a single giant mitochondrion? How does this relate to surface area?

Energy production occurs along the inner membrane. Many small mitochondria, each with folded cristae, give a far larger total surface area to volume ratio than one giant sphere of the same volume, so ATP can be produced much faster and more efficiently. Small mitochondria can also be positioned exactly where energy is needed and can divide to increase in number.

P7. If the skin cells start dividing by meiosis instead of mitosis, what will happen to a cut on the skin?

The cut would not heal properly. Meiosis produces cells that are genetically different and have only half the normal number of chromosomes. Repair needs exact genetic copies of the original skin cells with a full chromosome set, which only mitosis provides.
