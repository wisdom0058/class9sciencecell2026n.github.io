7. Cell Theory, Cell Lifespan and Cancer

Schleiden, Schwann, Virchow · the three statements · do cells live forever? · contact inhibition · cancer and tumours · programmed cell death · totipotency

2.5 Cell theory — the unifying principle of biology

1838 — German botanist Matthias Schleiden reported that all plants are made up of cells.
1839 — German zoologist Theodor Schwann found that all animals are also made up of cells.
1855 — German scientist Rudolf Virchow added that new cells are formed only from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula e cellula).

Together their work led to the classical Cell Theory:

  1. All living organisms are made up of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living beings.
  3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells.
Why is it called "unifying"? Because it applies to every living thing — from a single bacterium to a giant tree to a human. Whatever the organism, it is built of cells, works through cells, and its continuity through generations is explained by cell division. It joins all of biology into one framework.
Why was Virchow's addition important? Before him, many believed cells could form spontaneously from non-living fluid. Virchow's statement means every cell in your body can be traced back through divisions to the single fertilised egg you began as — and that life comes only from life.

2.5.1 Do cells grow and reproduce forever?

No. Normal cells grow and divide in a controlled way, stay in the right place, do their job, and eventually die when they are no longer needed. Dead cells are replaced by new ones that carry out the same function. So every cell has a definite lifespan — RBCs about 120 days, skin cells a few weeks, some nerve cells a lifetime. If cells do not die when they should, or die too early, problems arise.

Contact inhibition. In many animal cells, division stops when a cell comes into contact with neighbouring cells. This built-in "stop signal" keeps tissues in a single, orderly layer and prevents overcrowding.

Cancer — when control is lost

Sometimes the control system breaks down. Abnormal cells lose contact inhibition and keep growing and dividing uncontrollably. The pile of extra cells forms a tumour. Tumours may be benign (stay in one place, usually less dangerous) or malignant (cancerous). Cancerous tumours can invade nearby tissues and spread through blood or lymph to other parts of the body, where they form new tumours.

Can plants get tumours? Plant cells grow differently: because of their rigid cell walls they do not show contact inhibition and follow a different growth pattern. Plants can still develop tumour-like growths (galls, e.g., crown gall caused by the bacterium Agrobacterium), but because plant cells cannot move, such growths stay localised and do not spread like animal cancers.
Ready to go beyond — Programmed Cell Death (PCD). Cells also have natural ways of dying to keep a balance. PCD is a genetically regulated, organised process of selective cell destruction, essential for normal development, quality control and immune function. When an embryo develops, PCD removes the cells between the fingers — without it we would have webbed hands.

Totipotency and plant tissue culture

Meet a scientist. In 1902 the Austrian botanist Gottlieb Haberlandt proposed that any living plant cell — even a fully mature one from a permanent tissue — can develop into a complete plant if given suitable nutrients and favourable conditions. This ability of a plant cell to form all the different kinds of cells, and to change from one type to another, is called totipotency. His idea laid the foundation of Plant Tissue Culture Technology, used today to produce thousands of identical, disease-free plants (banana, orchids, sugarcane) from tiny pieces of tissue.

The hardworking cell — a closing thought

Even though cells are extremely tiny, they perform remarkable functions: they produce energy, synthesise and secrete substances, divide to form new cells, and work together to keep the whole body running. Every living being — from a tiny bacterium to a giant tree — is made of cells. You are one of the products of these tiny hardworking cells.

At a glance

  • The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms.
  • Prokaryotic cells: no well-defined nucleus (nucleoid), no membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotic cells: larger, true nucleus, many membrane-bound organelles.
  • All cells have a cell membrane; plants, fungi and bacteria also have a cell wall.
  • The nucleus holds chromosomes (DNA + proteins) carrying genetic information.
  • Key organelles: nucleus, ER, mitochondria, Golgi apparatus, ribosomes, lysosomes; plants also have plastids (chloroplasts, chromoplasts, leucoplasts) and a large vacuole.
  • Mitosis: 2 identical daughter cells. Meiosis: two-step, 4 daughter cells with half the chromosomes.
  • Normal cells grow in a controlled way and die naturally; cancer cells lose control and form tumours.

Q4. A cancerous tumour differs from a benign one in that it:
A. never grows
B. can invade nearby tissues and spread to other parts of the body
C. is always in plants
D. contains no cells
✔ Correct answer: B. can invade nearby tissues and spread to other parts of the body
Malignant tumours invade and spread (metastasis); benign ones stay localised.
Q5. Plant cells do not show contact inhibition because:
A. they have no nucleus
B. they have rigid cell walls and a different growth pattern
C. they do not divide
D. they lack DNA
✔ Correct answer: B. they have rigid cell walls and a different growth pattern
Rigid walls fix plant cells in place; they grow by a different pattern and their tumour-like growths stay localised.
Q6. Programmed Cell Death in an embryo is responsible for:
A. forming the heart
B. removing cells between the fingers so they separate
C. making red blood cells

✔ Correct answer: B. removing cells between the fingers so they separate
PCD removes the webbing between digits; without it we would have webbed hands.
Q7. The ability of a plant cell to develop into a complete plant is called:

Haberlandt (1902) proposed totipotency, the basis of plant tissue culture.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What are the three statements of the cell theory?

1. All living organisms are made of one or more cells. 2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function. 3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells.

Do cells live forever?

No. Every cell has a definite lifespan; it grows, works, and dies when no longer needed, and is replaced by new cells formed by division. Red blood cells live about 120 days; skin cells a few weeks.

What phenomenon prevents tumours in humans, and can plants get tumours?

Contact inhibition — normal cells stop dividing when they touch neighbours. Cancer cells lose it and form tumours. Plants lack contact inhibition because of rigid cell walls, but can form localised tumour-like galls (e.g., crown gall caused by Agrobacterium); these do not spread like animal cancers.

What is programmed cell death and why is it useful?

It is a genetically controlled, orderly process in which selected cells destroy themselves. It shapes organs during development (separating fingers), removes damaged or infected cells, and keeps the number of cells balanced.

What is totipotency?

The ability of a single plant cell, even a mature one, to divide and form all the cell types needed to make a complete plant, when given nutrients and suitable conditions. It is the basis of plant tissue culture.
