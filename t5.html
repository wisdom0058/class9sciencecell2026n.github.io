5. Cell Organelles — The Living Factory

Ribosomes · endoplasmic reticulum (RER/SER) · Golgi apparatus · lysosomes · mitochondria & ATP · plastids (chloroplast, chromoplast, leucoplast) · vacuoles

Ribosomes — the protein factories

Ribosomes are tiny granules found free in the cytoplasm or attached to the endoplasmic reticulum. They are the sites of protein synthesis — they read the instructions copied from DNA and join amino acids into proteins. Ribosomes are present in all cells, prokaryotic and eukaryotic; they are not membrane-bound.

Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) — the manufacturing factory

The ER is a large organelle that spreads like a network of membranes through the cytoplasm and is continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It synthesises and transports proteins, fats (lipids) and, in some specialised cells, hormones. Two types (Fig. 2.13):

  • Rough ER (RER) — looks rough under the electron microscope because ribosomes are attached to its surface. Mainly involved in protein synthesis and secretion — abundant in gland cells such as pancreatic cells that secrete digestive enzymes.
  • Smooth ER (SER) — no ribosomes, so it looks smooth. Involved in synthesis and storage of fats and hormones (e.g., in cells that make steroid hormones) and in detoxifying harmful chemicals.

Golgi apparatus — the packaging and shipping centre

Stacks of flattened, sac-like structures near the ER form the Golgi apparatus. It is functionally linked to the ER, the cell membrane and other organelles. It acts like the cell's post office: it modifies, sorts and packages proteins and lipids into small membrane bags called vesicles for transport within the cell, secretion out of the cell, or formation of lysosomes.

Meet a scientist. The Golgi apparatus was first observed in 1898 by the Italian scientist Camillo Golgi in the nerve cells of a barn owl, using special staining. Early microscopes could not resolve it clearly, so many doubted it existed — until electron microscopes confirmed it decades later. It was then named in his honour.

Lysosomes — the clean-up system

Cells produce waste materials and damaged, worn-out organelles. How do they avoid piling up? Lysosomes are single-membrane-bound sacs filled with enzymes that can break down unwanted proteins, carbohydrates, fats and even damaged parts of the cell, keeping it clean and healthy. The breakdown products are released into the cytoplasm and reused. Lysosomes also digest foreign agents such as bacteria that enter the cell.

Why must the enzymes be kept inside a membrane? Because they are powerful enough to digest the cell itself. The membrane keeps them safely separated. If many lysosomes burst, the cell digests itself — which is why lysosomes are sometimes called "suicide bags".
Threads of Curiosity. Human sperm cells carry lysosomal enzymes at their tip. When a sperm meets an egg, these enzymes break down the egg's outer layer so that fertilisation can take place.

Mitochondria — the powerhouse of the cell (Fig. 2.14)

Mitochondria (singular: mitochondrion) supply the energy for most cellular activities. Each is surrounded by two membranes:

  • the outer membrane is smooth and porous;
  • the inner membrane is folded into finger-like projections called cristae, which greatly increase the surface area for the chemical reactions that produce energy.

Inside, glucose and other food molecules are broken down using oxygen in cellular respiration. The energy released is stored in a molecule called ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate) — the cell's energy currency, spent on movement, transport, synthesis, division and everything else.

Why many small mitochondria and not one giant one? (Pause and Ponder 6) Energy production happens on the inner membrane. Many small mitochondria, each with folded cristae, have a far greater total surface area than one big sphere of the same volume, so they can make ATP much faster. They can also be placed exactly where energy is needed (e.g., near muscle fibres) and can divide to increase in number.

Plastids — centres for food synthesis and storage (plant cells)

Animals get food from their surroundings, but plants make it. Where? In special organelles called plastids, found only in plant cells (and algae). There are three kinds:

PlastidPigmentWhereFunction
ChloroplastGreen chlorophyllLeaves, green stemsPhotosynthesis — makes sugar using sunlight, CO₂ and water; stores it temporarily as starch
Chromoplast (chroma = colour)Yellow, orange, red pigmentsFlower petals, ripe fruits, carrotsBright colours attract pollinators and fruit-eating animals that disperse seeds
Leucoplast (leukos = white)None — colourlessRoots, tubers, seeds (potato, taro)Storage of starch, oils or proteins; classified by what they store

Structure of a chloroplast (Fig. 2.15): like a mitochondrion it is double-membrane-bound. Inside is a semi-fluid stroma. Within the stroma are stacks of disc-shaped membrane structures that contain chlorophyll; light energy is absorbed there. The sugars made are stored in the stroma along with starch granules.

Mitochondria and plastids resemble bacteria. Both have their own DNA and their own ribosomes, so they can make some of their own proteins, and both are double-membrane-bound. These features suggest they share an evolutionary history with single-celled organisms — ancient bacteria that were taken into early eukaryotic cells and stayed as partners.
Do white flowers contain any pigment? (Pause and Ponder 4) White petals generally have no coloured pigment — their plastids are leucoplasts. They look white because tiny air spaces between cells scatter all colours of light equally. (Some white flowers contain colourless pigments that reflect ultraviolet light, which insects can see.)
Do roots have plastids? Yes — not chloroplasts (no light underground, so no photosynthesis) but leucoplasts that store starch, as in potato tubers and taro. So plastids are present in all parts of the plant, just not the same type.

Vacuoles — storage and support

Where are water, minerals and waste stored? In a mature plant cell there is usually one large central vacuole, surrounded by a single selectively permeable membrane and filled with a watery fluid called cell sap. The vacuole stores water, minerals, sugars and waste. By holding a large amount of water it keeps the cell's contents pressed against the wall, maintaining pressure inside the cell — this keeps the plant cell firm.

Why do plants wilt without water? When water is short, the vacuole loses water, the pressure drops, cells become less firm, and leaves and stems droop — the plant wilts. Water the plant, the vacuoles refill by osmosis, and it stands up again.

In animal cells vacuoles are small and temporary, if present at all, and are used for short-term storage.

Q1. Which organelle is the site of protein synthesis?

B. ribosome
C. lysosome
D. vacuole

A. it is folded into cristae
B. ribosomes are attached to its surface
C. it stores starch
D. it has pores
✔ Correct answer: B. ribosomes are attached to its surface
Attached ribosomes give RER its rough appearance and its role in protein synthesis; SER has none.
Q3. The organelle that modifies, sorts and packages proteins into vesicles is the:
A. mitochondrion
B. Golgi apparatus
C. nucleolus
D. chloroplast

The Golgi apparatus is the cell's post office.
Q4. Lysosomes are called the clean-up system because they:
A. make ATP
B. contain enzymes that digest waste and worn-out parts
C. store water
D. carry pigments
✔ Correct answer: B. contain enzymes that digest waste and worn-out parts
Single-membrane sacs of enzymes break down unwanted materials; products are reused.
Q5. The folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane are called:
A. stroma
B. cristae
C. cell sap
D. chromatin
✔ Correct answer: B. cristae
Cristae increase surface area for the energy-releasing reactions of respiration.
Q6. The energy currency of the cell is:
A. DNA
B. glucose
C. ATP
D. chlorophyll
✔ Correct answer: C. ATP
Energy released in cellular respiration is stored in ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate).
Q7. Which plastid gives ripe tomatoes and carrots their colour?
A. chloroplast
B. chromoplast
C. leucoplast
D. lysosome
✔ Correct answer: B. chromoplast
Chromoplasts contain yellow, orange and red pigments.
Q8. Leucoplasts in potato tubers store:
A. chlorophyll
B. starch
C. oxygen
D. DNA only
✔ Correct answer: B. starch
Leucoplasts are colourless storage plastids; in potato they store starch.
Q9. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are thought to have evolved from bacteria because both:
A. have a cell wall
B. have their own DNA and ribosomes and a double membrane
C. are found only in animals
D. have a single membrane
✔ Correct answer: B. have their own DNA and ribosomes and a double membrane
Own DNA + own ribosomes + double membrane suggest a bacterial ancestry.
Q10. A plant wilts when its cells' vacuoles:
A. fill with water
B. lose water and pressure drops
C. turn green
D. divide
✔ Correct answer: B. lose water and pressure drops
Less water in the vacuole → less pressure against the wall → cells less firm → drooping.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between RER and SER?

RER has ribosomes on its surface (rough appearance) and makes and secretes proteins, e.g., in pancreatic cells. SER has no ribosomes (smooth), and makes and stores fats and hormones.

How are mitochondria and chloroplasts similar and different?

Similar: both double-membrane-bound, both have their own DNA and ribosomes, both handle energy. Different: mitochondria break down glucose to release energy as ATP (respiration) and are in all eukaryotic cells; chloroplasts use sunlight to make glucose (photosynthesis), contain chlorophyll, and are only in plant cells.

What would happen if all mitochondria were removed from a cell?

Cellular respiration would stop, so no ATP would be made. All energy-needing activities — synthesis, transport, movement, division — would halt, and the cell would soon die. A little energy can come from glycolysis in the cytoplasm, but far too little to sustain the cell.

Why are the colours of flowers and fruits useful to the plant?

Chromoplast pigments make petals bright to attract insects and birds for pollination, and make ripe fruits colourful to attract animals that eat them and disperse the seeds far away.

Which organelles build the cell membrane, and by what path?

Proteins: ribosomes on RER → RER → vesicles → Golgi (modified, packaged) → vesicles → fuse with the plasma membrane. Lipids: made in SER → Golgi → vesicles → plasma membrane. So ribosomes, RER, SER and Golgi together supply the membrane.
