1. The Cell — Basic Unit of Life, and How We Study It

Origin of life in water · unicellular & multicellular · tissues → organs · limit of resolution · the microscope · estimating cell size

Where did life begin?

Scientists widely agree that life originated in water. Some researchers think it did not begin in the vast oceans but in small water pools whose conditions kept changing — for example hot springs. In India, the hot springs of Puga Valley in Ladakh stay close to the boiling point of water even in a freezing climate. Such conditions resemble the early Earth about 3.5 billion years ago. The organisms living there today are mostly heat-loving bacteria called thermophiles (thermo = heat, phile = loving), and they are unicellular.

Scientists from the Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences, Lucknow, found that calcium carbonate forms rapidly around these springs. Such deposits may have protected early organic molecules from harmful radiation and extreme conditions, and may even have helped form the first protective membrane — the barrier that defines a cell.

Why does a membrane "define" a cell? A cell is a tiny bag of chemicals that must stay together and stay different from its surroundings. The membrane is the boundary that keeps the inside separate from the outside, while still allowing selected exchange. Without a boundary there is no "inside" — and so no cell.

All living things are made of cells

The cell is the basic level at which life exists. Some organisms consist of only one cell — they are unicellular (bacteria, yeast, Amoeba). Others, such as plants, fish, birds and humans, are made of millions or trillions of cells working together — they are multicellular.

In a multicellular body the cells are organised in levels:

Cell — the smallest living unit.
Tissue — a group of similar cells performing a similar function (e.g., muscle tissue).
Organ — different tissues organised together (e.g., lungs).
Organ system — several organs working together. Example: nasal pores + nasal cavity + trachea + lungs = the respiratory system.

Even when cells are organised into tissues, organs and systems, the cell remains the fundamental unit of structure and function. That raises big questions: how do such tiny cells do so many jobs? What parts do they have? How do they communicate? Do they live forever? This chapter answers them.

2.1 How to study cells? — the limit of resolution

Draw two tiny dots on paper and slowly bring them closer. At some point your eye can no longer see them as two separate dots — they merge into one. The ability to see two very close objects as separate and distinct is called resolution. When viewed from about 25 cm (the near point of the human eye), two points can be told apart only if they are at least about 0.1 mm apart. So the limit of resolution of the human eye is 0.1 mm.

Most cells are far smaller than 0.1 mm (Fig. 2.1) — a typical plant or animal cell is 10–100 micrometres (µm), and 1 mm = 1000 µm. That is why cells are invisible to the unaided eye, and why we need a microscope.

Magnification vs resolution. Magnification makes an object appear larger. Resolution is the ability to see fine detail as separate. Contrast is the difference in brightness between parts of an object. Scientists improved microscopes by improving all three.

The light microscope

A convex lens makes an object look bigger. A microscope uses a combination of two convex lenses — the objective lens (near the object, e.g., 10X or 40X) and the eyepiece (near your eye, usually 10X). Light from the mirror passes up through the specimen on the slide resting on the stage, through the objective and the body tube to the eyepiece. The coarse adjustment knob moves the tube quickly to bring the image roughly into focus; the fine adjustment knob sharpens it. The handle (arm) and base support everything (Fig. 2.2).

Robert Hooke was the first to see cells, in 1665, with a microscope he designed himself (about 200–300X). Looking at a thin slice of cork, he saw tiny box-like compartments and called them "cells" (from cellula, a small room).

Total magnification = magnifying power of eyepiece × magnifying power of objective. With a 10X eyepiece and a 10X objective, the total is 10 × 10 = 100X: the object appears 100 times larger. With a 40X objective it becomes 400X.

Electron microscopes use a beam of electrons instead of light. Because electrons can resolve far finer detail, they show cell structure at the nanometre scale (1 nm = one-billionth of a metre) — organelles, ribosomes, even the membrane itself. Activity 2.1 — estimating the size of a cell

Place a transparent ruler with mm markings on the stage, focus, and measure the diameter of the circular field of view in mm through the eyepiece.
Convert to micrometres: 1 mm = 1000 µm. If the field is 5 mm wide, that is 5 × 1000 = 5000 µm.
Replace the ruler with an onion peel slide. Count how many cells lie in one straight line along the diameter.
Estimated size of one cell = diameter of field in µmnumber of cells along the diameter
Suppose 25 cells fit across. Then one cell ≈ 5000 µm25 = 200 µm.
Why does this work? The cells lie side by side across the field like beads on a string. The total width of the field is shared equally among them, so dividing the field width by the number of cells gives the average width of one cell.

If the microscope is at 100X, this 200 µm cell appears 100 times larger — 20 000 µm, i.e., 2 cm across in the eyepiece — while its real size is still 200 µm.

More on the History of Cell Discovery & Cell Sizes

  • Robert Hooke (1665) — coined the term cell (Latin cellula, "little room") in his book Micrographia, observing dead cork cells.
  • Anton van Leeuwenhoek (1674–80) — a Dutch merchant who built simple but powerful microscopes and was the first to describe living cells: bacteria, protozoa, sperm cells and red blood cells ("animalcules").
  • Robert Brown (1831) — discovered the nucleus in orchid root cells.
  • Purkinje (1839) — named the living substance of the cell protoplasm ("first substance").
  • Schleiden (1838) & Schwann (1839) — cell theory; Virchow (1855) — all cells come from pre-existing cells.
  • Knoll & Ruska (1932) — designed the electron microscope.

Cell size and shape vary with function. The smallest cells are mycoplasma (about 0.1–0.5 µm); a typical cell is 10–100 µm; the human egg is about 0.1 mm (just visible); the largest cell is the ostrich egg (about 15 cm without shell); the longest cells are nerve cells (up to 1 m). Nerve cells are elongated, muscle cells spindle-shaped, pigment cells branched, RBCs disc-shaped — each shape suits its job. Cytology is the branch of biology that studies cells. A compound microscope also has stage clips to hold the slide and a condenser below the stage to concentrate light.

Q3. A microscope has a 10X eyepiece and a 40X objective. Its total magnification is:
A. 50X
B. 400X
C. 4X
D. 140X
✔ Correct answer: B. 400X
Total magnification = eyepiece × objective = 10 × 40 = 400X.
Q4. The field of view is 4 mm wide and 20 onion cells fit across it. Estimated cell size:
A. 20 µm
B. 200 µm
C. 2000 µm
D. 0.2 µm
✔ Correct answer: B. 200 µm
4 mm = 4000 µm; 4000 ÷ 20 = 200 µm.
Q5. An electron microscope gives finer detail than a light microscope because it uses:
A. brighter light
B. a beam of electrons instead of light
C. bigger glass lenses
D. coloured stains
✔ Correct answer: B. a beam of electrons instead of light
Electrons can resolve details at the nanometre scale, far beyond visible light.
Q6. A group of similar cells performing a similar function is called:

B. a tissue
C. an organ system
D. an organism
✔ Correct answer: B. a tissue
Cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Why can't we see cells with our eyes?

The human eye can separate two points only if they are at least 0.1 mm apart. Most cells are 10–100 µm, which is 0.01–0.1 mm — at or below this limit — so they blur into nothing. A microscope magnifies them and also resolves their details.

What is the difference between magnification and resolution?

Magnification is how many times larger an object appears. Resolution is the ability to see two close points as separate — that is, to see fine detail. A blurry image can be magnified a lot but still show no detail; good resolution is what makes the detail visible.

How do we find total magnification of a microscope?

Multiply the magnifying power of the eyepiece by that of the objective lens. 10X × 10X = 100X; 10X × 40X = 400X.

Why use an onion peel to study cells?

The inner skin of an onion scale is a single layer of large (about 200 µm), regularly arranged, box-shaped cells. It peels off easily, is transparent, and shows the cell wall and nucleus clearly after staining with safranin.

What are the main parts of a light microscope?

Eyepiece, body tube, objective lenses (10X, 40X), stage (holds the slide), mirror (reflects light up through the specimen), coarse and fine adjustment knobs (for focusing), handle or arm, and base.
