3. The Cell Wall — Plant Cells vs Animal Cells

Activity 2.3 — comparing plant and animal cells

Peel a thin layer from an onion scale or a Rhoeo (cradle lily) leaf, mount it on a slide with a drop of safranin stain and a cover slip.
Gently scrape the inside of your cheek with a cotton swab or the blunt end of a toothpick; spread the cheek cells on a slide, add a drop of water and a few drops of methylene blue, and cover.
Observation: onion/Rhoeo cells (Fig. 2.8a) are box-shaped and regularly arranged like bricks in a wall. Cheek cells (Fig. 2.8b) are irregular in shape and loosely arranged.
Why the difference? Plant cells have a rigid cell wall outside the membrane that fixes their shape and holds them in neat rows. Animal cells have only the flexible membrane, so they take irregular shapes.

Now put 20% sugar solution on both slides and wait half an hour. The plant cells' outer boundary stays the same, but the inner content shrinks and a gap appears between the inner and outer boundaries (Fig. 2.9). The cheek cells, however, shrink considerably as a whole.

Fig. 2.9 & Activity 2.3 — in 20% sugar solution the plant cell keeps its box shape (rigid cell wall) while its contents shrink away from the wall; the animal cell, with no wall, shrinks as a whole.

2.2.2 Cell wall — the outer covering of plant cells

Plants generally cannot move from place to place. They must stand in one spot and withstand wind, rain and their own weight. So plant cells have an extra covering outside the cell membrane — the cell wall. It:

  • gives the cell a fixed shape and mechanical strength;
  • helps leaves and flowers stay firm and keeps the plant upright;
  • protects the cell from bursting when it takes in a lot of water.

Although rigid, the cell wall is fully permeable — water and dissolved minerals pass through it freely. (Compare: the membrane is selectively permeable.) The permeable wall plus the selective membrane together let roots absorb water and nutrients from soil.

Why does the plant cell keep its shape in sugar solution while its contents shrink? In the concentrated solution the cells lose water by osmosis. The membrane and cytoplasm shrink and pull away from the wall, but the rigid wall does not collapse, so the outer boundary remains the same. This shows that the wall maintains the plant cell's shape. Animal cells (cheek cells) have no wall, so when they lose water the whole cell shrinks.

Without a rigid wall, animal cells can change shape easily. This flexibility supports movement and the functioning of animal tissues — think of muscle cells contracting or white blood cells squeezing between other cells.

What is the cell wall made of?

The plant cell wall is made mainly of cellulose — a carbohydrate formed by many glucose units linked together in long chains. Humans cannot digest cellulose; in our diet it acts as roughage (fibre), which adds bulk and helps the movement of food through the gut, preventing constipation.

Fungi and bacteria also have cell walls (made of different materials) for protection and support. Animal cells never have a cell wall.

Pause and Ponder: (1) Plants are fixed in one place and must resist wind and rain — they need a rigid wall; animals move and need flexible cells. (2) If the plant cell wall became as flexible as the membrane, cells could not keep their shape, the plant would droop and collapse, and cells might burst when they absorb water.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Why do plant cells need a cell wall but animal cells do not?

Plants are fixed in one place and must resist wind, rain and gravity, so their cells need a rigid wall for shape and strength. Animals move, and their cells must be flexible to allow contraction, movement and changes of shape, so a rigid wall would be a disadvantage.

What would happen if a plant cell wall became as flexible as the membrane?

The cells would not hold their shape; the plant could not stay upright and would droop. Cells taking in water by osmosis would swell and might burst, because nothing would resist the inward flow.

Why does a plant cell not burst in pure water while an animal cell may?

As water enters, the plant cell's contents press against the strong cell wall, which pushes back and stops further entry — the cell just becomes firm (turgid). An animal cell has only a thin membrane, so it keeps swelling and can burst.

What stains are used for onion peel and cheek cells, and why?

Safranin (red) for onion or Rhoeo peel and methylene blue for cheek cells. Cells are almost transparent; stains colour the nucleus and cell boundaries so they can be seen clearly under the microscope.
