4. Inside the Cell — Prokaryotes, Eukaryotes and the Nucleus

2.3 The cell interior — a coordinated working system

Most cells have three basic parts:

  • a selectively permeable plasma membrane;
  • a semi-fluid, jelly-like cytoplasm in which everything else floats and where many chemical reactions occur;
  • a prominent nucleus.

Besides the nucleus, the cytoplasm contains several small sub-cellular components called organelles ("little organs"), most visible only with an electron microscope.

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells (Table 2.2)

A bacterial cell has no well-defined nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (organelles surrounded by their own membranes). Such cells are prokaryotic (pro = primitive, karyon = nucleus). In them, most cellular activities take place directly in the cytoplasm. Their DNA is a single circular molecule lying in a region called the nucleoid.

Plant and animal cells have a true nucleus enclosed by a nuclear membrane, plus many membrane-bound organelles. They are eukaryotic (eu = true, karyon = nucleus).

Why do eukaryotic cells need organelles? (2.3.1) A eukaryotic cell is large and does many things at once. Organelles are like separate rooms in a factory: each has its own membrane, its own conditions and its own job — building new materials, removing waste, providing energy — so different processes can run independently at the same time without interfering with one another. A cell is a tiny living factory in which every part does a specific job.

In eukaryotic cells, a network of fine fibres called the cytoskeleton gives structural support, maintains shape and enables cell movement and internal transport (seen only under an electron microscope). The cytoplasm may also hold cell inclusions such as stored starch (plant cells) or crystals of calcium oxalate or silica.

Ready to go beyond — acellular agents. Viruses (genetic material in a protein coat), viroids (genetic material without a protein coat) and prions (misfolded proteins with no genetic material) are not cells. They are too small for a light microscope and can multiply only inside living cells.

Nucleus — house of coded instructions (Fig. 2.11)

  • The nucleus is bounded by a double-layered nuclear membrane with nuclear pores that allow materials to pass between the nucleus and the cytoplasm.
  • Inside is the nucleolus, a dense round body where the subunits of ribosomes are made. These subunits leave through the pores, and in the cytoplasm one large and one small subunit join to form a working ribosome.
  • The nucleus contains chromosomes, which carry the information for the inheritance of characters from parents to the next generation, in the form of DNA (Deoxyribonucleic acid). Chromosomes are made of DNA + specific proteins.
  • The functional segments of DNA are called genes — each gene is an instruction for one feature or one protein.

Chromatin vs chromosomes (Fig. 2.12): in a cell that is not dividing, the DNA is spread out as a tangled mass of thread-like chromatin. When the cell is about to divide, the chromatin coils up tightly into distinct rod-shaped chromosomes that become visible under the microscope. Sequence: Cell → Nucleus → Chromatin → Chromosome → DNA → Gene.

Why is the nucleus called the control centre? Because the DNA inside it carries the instructions for making every protein the cell needs, and proteins carry out or control almost every activity. Copy the instructions correctly, and the daughter cells work correctly.
Threads of Curiosity — cells without a nucleus. Mature human red blood cells (RBCs) are enucleate (no nucleus). This leaves more room for haemoglobin, so each RBC carries more oxygen. But without a nucleus they cannot repair or divide themselves, so they live only about 120 days. Other examples: sieve tube cells in plants and platelets in blood.
Threads of Curiosity — synthetic DNA (2010). J. Craig Venter's team read the complete DNA of the bacterium Mycoplasma mycoides, chemically synthesised an exact copy, removed the DNA from a related bacterium and inserted the synthetic DNA. The cell then grew and divided following the new instructions — proving that DNA controls the structure and activities of a cell. Note: only the DNA was synthetic; the rest of the cell came from an existing living cell.

Extra: More Differences & Structures

Prokaryotic cells may have a slimy capsule outside the wall, whip-like flagella for movement and hair-like pili. Their respiration happens on the plasma membrane (mesosomes), their ribosomes are the smaller 70S type (eukaryotic cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S; mitochondria and chloroplasts also have 70S ribosomes — more evidence of their bacterial origin), and their single circular chromosome has no histone proteins. Prokaryotes divide by simple fission, not mitosis.

Eukaryotic extras: Centrioles (in a centrosome near the nucleus) occur in animal cells and help organise cell division; they are absent in higher plants. Plasmodesmata are fine cytoplasmic channels through plant cell walls that connect neighbouring cells. Peroxisomes are small single-membrane sacs that break down harmful hydrogen peroxide. The fluid inside the nucleus is the nucleoplasm; the membrane around a plant vacuole is the tonoplast. Organelles with two membranes: nucleus, mitochondria, plastids. Single membrane: ER, Golgi, lysosomes, vacuoles, peroxisomes. No membrane: ribosomes, centrioles.

Q1. Which of these is NOT one of the three basic parts of most cells?
A. plasma membrane
B. cytoplasm
C. nucleus
D. cell wall
✔ Correct answer: D. cell wall
The three basic parts are the plasma membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus. The cell wall is an extra covering in plants, fungi and bacteria.
Q2. Prokaryotic cells differ from eukaryotic cells in that they:
A. have a true nucleus
B. lack a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles
C. are always larger
D. have chloroplasts
✔ Correct answer: B. lack a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles
Pro = primitive: DNA lies free in a nucleoid; no membrane-bound organelles; typically 1–10 µm.
Q3. The region of a bacterial cell containing its circular DNA is the:
A. nucleolus
B. nucleoid
C. nuclear pore
D. vacuole
✔ Correct answer: B. nucleoid
The nucleoid is the DNA-containing region without a surrounding membrane.
Q4. Ribosomal subunits are made in the:
A. nucleolus
B. mitochondria
C. Golgi apparatus
D. lysosome
✔ Correct answer: A. nucleolus
The nucleolus is the dense body in the nucleus where ribosomal subunits are synthesised.
Q5. Chromosomes become visible as rod-shaped structures:
A. at all times
B. only when the cell is about to divide
C. only in bacteria
D. only in RBCs
✔ Correct answer: B. only when the cell is about to divide
In a non-dividing cell the DNA is spread out as chromatin; it condenses into chromosomes before division.
Q6. Mature human red blood cells lack a nucleus. This allows them to:
A. divide faster
B. carry more haemoglobin and thus more oxygen
C. live longer
D. make more proteins
✔ Correct answer: B. carry more haemoglobin and thus more oxygen
More space for haemoglobin means more oxygen transport, but they cannot divide or repair and live only ~120 days.
Q7. Genes are:
A. whole chromosomes
B. functional segments of DNA
C. proteins in the membrane
D. ribosomes
✔ Correct answer: B. functional segments of DNA
A gene is a functional segment of DNA carrying the instruction for one feature/protein.
Q8. A typical eukaryotic cell has a diameter of about:
A. 1–10 nm
B. 1–10 µm
C. 10–100 µm
D. 1–10 mm
✔ Correct answer: C. 10–100 µm
Eukaryotic cells are typically 10–100 µm; prokaryotic cells are 1–10 µm.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between chromatin and chromosome?

They are the same material — DNA with proteins — in two states. In a non-dividing cell it is loosely spread as thread-like chromatin. Before division it coils tightly into compact rod-shaped chromosomes, which are easier to move and share equally between daughter cells.

Why is a virus not considered a cell?

A virus is only genetic material wrapped in a protein coat. It has no membrane of its own, no cytoplasm, no organelles, and cannot grow, respire or reproduce by itself — it multiplies only by using a living host cell's machinery. So it is acellular.

What do nuclear pores do?

They are openings in the double nuclear membrane through which materials are exchanged between the nucleus and the cytoplasm — for example, ribosomal subunits move out and building blocks move in.

Are there other cells besides RBCs without a nucleus?

Yes. Blood platelets are cell fragments without a nucleus, and mature sieve tube cells in plant phloem lose their nucleus; they are kept alive by neighbouring companion cells.

What is the cytoskeleton?

A network of fine protein fibres inside eukaryotic cells that gives support, maintains shape, and helps in cell movement and transport of materials within the cell. It is seen only with an electron microscope.
