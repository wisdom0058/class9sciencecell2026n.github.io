Q1. In the hot springs of the Puga Valley in Ladakh, temperatures remain nearly at the boiling point of water despite the cold climate. Which type of organisms predominantly inhabit these extreme conditions?
A. Multicellular green algae
B. Complex eukaryotic fungi
C. Heat-loving bacteria called thermophiles
D. Cold-adapted specialised protozoans
✔ Correct answer: C. Heat-loving bacteria called thermophiles
Hot springs in the Puga Valley stay very hot even in a cold climate. The organisms living there are mostly heat-loving unicellular bacteria called thermophiles, which survive in conditions similar to early Earth and help scientists understand the origin of cellular life.
Q2. What is the limit of resolution of the human eye when viewing two tiny objects from a distance of 25 cm?
A. 1.0 mm
B. 0.1 mm
C. 0.01 mm
D. 10 µm
✔ Correct answer: B. 0.1 mm
The limit of resolution is the minimum distance at which two close points are still seen as distinct. From 25 cm, points about 0.1 mm apart can be seen separately; closer than that they merge into one.
Q3. (Diagram: animal cells that have become shrunken and crinkled.) Based on the visual change, what is the nature of the surrounding medium?
A. Hypotonic — water enters and cells swell
B. Hypertonic — water leaves and cells shrink
C. Isotonic — no net water movement
D. Saturated — solute enters and cells shrink
✔ Correct answer: B. Hypertonic — water leaves and cells shrink
Shrinking cells mean water is moving out. This happens when the outside solution has a higher solute concentration than the cell interior — a hypertonic solution. Hypotonic causes swelling; isotonic keeps shape unchanged.
Q4. Who first observed cell structures with a self-designed microscope while examining a thin slice of cork in 1665?
A. Theodor Schwann
B. Matthias Schleiden
C. Rudolf Virchow
D. Robert Hooke
✔ Correct answer: D. Robert Hooke
In 1665 Robert Hooke examined thin cork slices under his own microscope, saw small box-like compartments resembling tiny rooms and coined the term 'cells'.
Q5. A student observes an onion peel. The circular field of view is 6000 µm in diameter and exactly 30 cells lie along it. Estimated size of one onion cell?
A. 20 µm
B. 200 µm
C. 180 µm
D. 500 µm
✔ Correct answer: B. 200 µm
Cell size = field diameter ÷ number of cells along the diameter = 6000 ÷ 30 = 200 µm.
Q6. Assertion (A): The cell membrane is also called a selectively permeable membrane.
Reason (R): It allows all substances to pass freely in and out without restriction.
A. Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
B. Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A
C. A is true but R is false
D. A is false but R is true
✔ Correct answer: C. A is true but R is false
The membrane is called selectively permeable because it allows some substances through and blocks others. So A is true, but R is false.
Q7. According to the fluid-mosaic model, which component acts as the 'gatekeeper' helping selected substances pass through the membrane?
A. The water-attracting lipid heads
B. The water-repelling lipid tails
C. The specialised embedded membrane proteins
D. Rigid crystalline cellulose fibres
✔ Correct answer: C. The specialised embedded membrane proteins
The membrane is a lipid bilayer (heads outward, tails inward) with proteins embedded in it. These proteins act as gatekeepers regulating the passage of substances.
Q8. Four onion-peel slides: A — field 2000 µm, 10 cells; B — 1500 µm, 5 cells; C — 4000 µm, 40 cells; D — 3000 µm, 12 cells. Which slide shows the largest estimated cell size?
A. Slide A
B. Slide B
C. Slide C
D. Slide D
✔ Correct answer: B. Slide B
Size = diameter ÷ cells. A = 200 µm; B = 1500 ÷ 5 = 300 µm; C = 100 µm; D = 250 µm. Slide B is largest.
Q9. A freshly cut raw potato piece is submerged in 20% salt solution for two hours. What happens and why?
A. It swells — medium is hypotonic
B. It shrinks — medium is hypertonic
C. No change — medium is isotonic
D. It bursts due to inward osmotic pressure
✔ Correct answer: B. It shrinks — medium is hypertonic
20% salt solution is hypertonic relative to potato cells. Water moves out through the selectively permeable membranes by osmosis, so the piece shrinks.
Q10. Statements: 1. A cell in an isotonic solution shows no net water movement. 2. A cell in a hypotonic solution loses water and shrinks. 3. A cell in a hypertonic solution absorbs water and swells. Which is/are correct?
A. 1 only
B. 1 and 2 only
C. 2 and 3 only
D. 1, 2 and 3
✔ Correct answer: A. 1 only
Isotonic → no net movement (correct). Hypotonic → cell gains water and swells, so 2 is wrong. Hypertonic → cell loses water and shrinks, so 3 is wrong.
Q11. The net movement of particles from higher to lower concentration occurs spontaneously. What is the term when this involves diffusion of water strictly across a selectively permeable membrane?
A. Active transport
B. Facilitated diffusion
C. Osmosis
D. Endocytosis
✔ Correct answer: C. Osmosis
Diffusion needs no membrane. Osmosis is specifically the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane along a concentration gradient.
Q12. (Diagram: a bacterial cell; region 'X' is an irregular area of DNA in the cytoplasm with no membrane.) Which option correctly identifies X?
A. Nucleolus — dense body making ribosomal subunits
B. Nucleoid — undefined region containing genetic material without a membrane
C. Nucleus — double-membrane organelle with chromosomes
D. Cytoplasm — jelly-like site of reactions
✔ Correct answer: B. Nucleoid — undefined region containing genetic material without a membrane
The cell is a prokaryote. X is the nucleoid, where the single circular DNA lies freely without a nuclear envelope. True nucleus and nucleolus occur only in eukaryotes.
Q13. Arrange in increasing order of size: 1. Typical bacterial cell 2. Atoms 3. Simple proteins 4. Viruses 5. Mature human cell

Atoms (0.1 nm) → proteins (nm) → viruses (tens of nm) → bacteria (1–10 µm) → human cell (10–100 µm).
Q14. Which statement accurately describes a functional characteristic of the plant cell wall?
A. It completely blocks entry of water and minerals
B. It synthesises lipids and cellulose
C. It is permeable, allowing water and dissolved minerals to pass easily

✔ Correct answer: C. It is permeable, allowing water and dissolved minerals to pass easily
The cell wall gives rigid support but is fully permeable; water and dissolved minerals pass freely. Selective permeability belongs to the membrane beneath it.
Q15. Match List-I with List-II. P. Golgi apparatus; Q. Lysosomes; R. Mitochondria; S. Nucleus — 1. Clean-up system with digestive enzymes; 2. House of coded genetic instructions; 3. Site of energy production; 4. Packaging and shipping centre

Q16. Identical potato pieces (initial mass 5.0 g) in four sugar solutions. After 2 h: P = 5.0 g, Q = 6.2 g, R = 3.8 g, S = 4.5 g. Which beaker had a hypotonic solution?
A. Beaker P
B. Beaker Q
C. Beaker R
D. Beaker S
✔ Correct answer: B. Beaker Q
In a hypotonic medium water enters the cells and mass increases. Only Q gained mass (5.0 → 6.2 g). P is isotonic; R and S are hypertonic.
Q17. Bacterial cells lack a true membrane-bound nucleus; their genetic material is a single circular DNA molecule. What is this undefined genetic region called?

✔ Correct answer: B. Nucleoid
In prokaryotes the DNA lies freely in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid.
Q18. (Diagram: ER connected to a stack of flattened sacs labelled 'Y'.) What is the primary function of organelle Y?

✔ Correct answer: D. Modifies, sorts and packages proteins into vesicles
Y is the Golgi apparatus (stacks of flattened sacs). It processes proteins from the ER and packages them into vesicles. Lipids → SER; ATP → mitochondria; waste → lysosomes.

Q22. (Diagram: mitochondrion; 'Z' marks the folds of the inner membrane.) What is the significance of the folding marked Z?

✔ Correct answer: B. Increases internal surface area to maximise energy production
Z points to cristae — finger-like folds of the inner membrane that greatly increase surface area for the reactions of cellular respiration, maximising ATP production.

B. Very Short Answer Questions

1. What is the limit of resolution?

The minimum distance between two points at which they can still be seen as distinct, separate objects (about 0.1 mm for the human eye at 25 cm).

2. Who discovered the cell and when?

Robert Hooke, in 1665, while examining a thin slice of cork under his self-made microscope.

3. State one difference between a light microscope and an electron microscope.

A light microscope uses visible light and glass lenses to magnify objects; an electron microscope uses a beam of electrons and reveals much finer detail (nanometre scale).

4. Why is the cell membrane called selectively permeable?

Because it allows only certain substances to pass through while blocking the movement of others.

5. What is the fluid-mosaic model of the cell membrane?

It describes the membrane as a flexible (fluid) lipid bilayer with protein molecules embedded in it like tiles of a mosaic.

6. Define osmosis in the context of a cell.

The net movement of water molecules through the selectively permeable cell membrane from a region of higher water concentration to one of lower water concentration.

7. What would happen if an animal cell is placed in a hypertonic solution?

It would lose water to the surroundings by osmosis and shrink.

8. What is the composition of the plant cell wall?

Mainly cellulose — a complex carbohydrate made of many glucose units linked together.

9. What is the main function of the cell wall in plants?

It provides rigid structural support, keeps leaves and flowers firm, and helps the plant withstand stresses like strong wind.

10. How does a prokaryotic cell differ from a eukaryotic cell regarding the nuclear region?

A prokaryotic cell has no nuclear membrane — only a nucleoid; a eukaryotic cell has a true nucleus enclosed by a double membrane.

11. What is a nucleoid?

The undefined region in a prokaryotic cell that contains the genetic material as a single circular DNA molecule, without any membrane.

12. What is the significance of genes inside the nucleus?

Genes are functional segments of DNA that carry the information for inheriting characters from parents to the next generation.

13. Name the dense round body in the nucleus and state its role.

The nucleolus; it synthesises the subunits of ribosomes, which move out into the cytoplasm.

14. Why does the rough ER appear rough under a microscope?

Because numerous ribosomes are attached to its surface.

15. State one important function of the smooth ER.

Synthesis and storage of fats (lipids) and hormones.

16. What is the main result of the Golgi apparatus functioning in a cell?

Proteins and lipids are modified, sorted and packaged into vesicles for transport, secretion or lysosome formation.

17. Why are lysosomes called the clean-up system of the cell?

They contain powerful digestive enzymes that break down waste, damaged cell parts and foreign particles, keeping the cell clean.

18. Describe the structure of a mitochondrion in one sentence.

A mitochondrion has a smooth porous outer membrane and a highly folded inner membrane with finger-like projections called cristae.

19. What is the scientific term for the energy currency of the cell?

Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP).

20. State one structural similarity between mitochondria and plastids.

Both possess their own DNA and ribosomes (and both are bounded by two membranes).

21. What is the functional role of leucoplasts in plant cells?

They are colourless plastids that store food materials such as starch, oils and proteins.

22. What causes the bright yellow or orange colours in flower petals?

Chromoplasts — plastids containing yellow, orange or red pigments that attract pollinators.

23. What is the function of the large central vacuole in mature plant cells?

It stores water, minerals, sugars and waste (cell sap) and maintains turgor pressure that keeps the cell firm.

24. Give the condition under which a plant becomes wilted.

When it does not get enough water — the vacuoles lose water, pressure drops, cells become less firm and the plant droops.

25. What is the direct outcome of mitosis?

Two genetically identical daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent cell.

26. Where does meiosis primarily take place in higher animals?

In the reproductive organs — testes in males and ovaries in females.

27. What is the biological significance of meiosis?

It halves the chromosome number to form gametes and creates genetic variation, essential for sexual reproduction.

28. What would you observe if normal cells lose their contact inhibition?

They would keep dividing uncontrollably and form a mass of cells — a tumour, which may become cancerous.

29. State one major principle of the classical Cell Theory.

All living organisms are made up of one or more cells (also: the cell is the basic unit of life; all cells arise from pre-existing cells).

30. What is meant by Programmed Cell Death?

A genetically regulated process of selective cell destruction essential for normal development, quality control and immune function.

C. Short Answer Questions

SA1. Explain the fluid mosaic model of the cell membrane.

The model describes the membrane as a flexible lipid bilayer: two layers of lipid molecules with water-attracting heads facing outwards (towards the watery outside and cytoplasm) and water-repelling tails pointing inwards. Proteins are embedded in this bilayer. The lipids and proteins can move sideways, rotate and flip, so the membrane behaves like a fluid; the proteins are scattered like tiles in a mosaic — hence the name. The proteins act as gatekeepers, letting specific substances pass through the selectively permeable membrane.

SA2. State the classical Cell Theory. Name the scientists who contributed to its formulation.

The classical Cell Theory states: (1) all living organisms are made up of one or more cells; (2) the cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life; (3) all new cells arise from pre-existing cells. It was proposed jointly by Matthias Schleiden (German botanist, 1838) and Theodor Schwann (German zoologist, 1839). Rudolf Virchow (1855) expanded it with the idea that new cells form by division of pre-existing cells.

SA3. Draw a well-labelled diagram of a mitochondrion. Explain why it is called the powerhouse of the cell.

(Diagram: oval organelle with outer membrane, inner membrane folded into cristae, intermembrane space, matrix containing DNA and ribosomes — see the animation on Topic 5.)

Mitochondria generate the energy needed for most cellular activities. Inside them, glucose and other food molecules are broken down with oxygen during cellular respiration, releasing energy that is captured in ATP. The cell uses this ATP for synthesis, transport, movement and division. The folded inner membrane (cristae) provides a large surface for these energy-generating reactions — hence 'powerhouse'.

SA4. What is osmosis? How does it differ from diffusion?

Osmosis is the spontaneous movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration to lower water concentration through a selectively permeable membrane. Diffusion is the net movement of any particles from higher to lower concentration along a concentration gradient. Differences: osmosis involves only the solvent (water) and strictly requires a selectively permeable membrane; diffusion can involve solids, liquids or gases, can occur in any medium, and needs no membrane.

SA5. Study the experimental setup: Beaker A — potato piece in plain water (swollen); Beaker B — potato piece in 20% salt solution (shrunken). Identify the type of solutions and explain the change in size.

Beaker A contains a hypotonic solution; Beaker B a hypertonic solution. In A the water concentration outside is higher than inside the potato cells, so water moves into the cells by osmosis — the potato swells. In B the salt solution has a lower water concentration than the cells, so water moves out of the potato cells into the solution — the cells lose water and the potato shrinks.

SA6. A student claims that plant cells will burst when placed in a highly dilute medium, just like animal cells. Do you agree? Justify.

No. Animal cells lack a rigid covering, so in a dilute (hypotonic) medium they swell and may burst. Plant cells have a rigid cellulose cell wall outside the membrane. When water enters, the contents swell and press against the wall, and the wall exerts an equal and opposite pressure that stops further intake. So the plant cell becomes firm (turgid) but does not burst.

SA7. Compare prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells with respect to size, nucleus and membrane-bound organelles.

Size: prokaryotic 1–10 µm; eukaryotic 10–100 µm. Nucleus: prokaryotes lack a well-defined nucleus — DNA lies free in the nucleoid; eukaryotes have a true nucleus enclosed by a double nuclear membrane. Organelles: prokaryotes have no membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotes have many (mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, plastids).

SA8. Explain the role of lysosomes as the clean-up system of the cell and how they manage cellular waste.

Lysosomes are single-membrane sacs filled with powerful digestive enzymes. When waste, worn-out organelles or foreign material (e.g., bacteria) accumulate, lysosomes engulf them and their enzymes break the complex proteins, carbohydrates and fats into simpler substances. These products are released into the cytoplasm for reuse. By continuously removing non-functional parts, lysosomes keep the cell clean, healthy and efficient.

SA9. Describe the process of cell division by mitosis and mention the genetic nature of the daughter cells.

Before mitosis the parent cell duplicates its entire DNA. The chromatin condenses into distinct rod-shaped chromosomes, each with two copies. The copies are pulled apart equally to the two ends of the cell, and finally the cytoplasm splits. The result is two genetically identical daughter cells, each with exactly the same DNA and the same chromosome number as the parent — ensuring genetic stability for growth and repair.

SA10. Draw a labelled diagram of the pathway for protein processing involving the ER and Golgi apparatus. How do these two organelles work together?

(See the animated pathway diagram on Topic 5: Ribosome/RER → vesicle → Golgi → vesicle → plasma membrane / lysosome.)

Ribosomes on the RER synthesise proteins. The proteins are sent in small vesicles to the Golgi apparatus — the packaging and shipping centre. The Golgi receives, modifies and sorts them into new vesicles, which deliver the processed proteins to their destinations: the plasma membrane for secretion, or organelles such as lysosomes.

SA11. What would happen if a cell loses its natural contact inhibition property? Explain the consequences for the organism.

Contact inhibition makes normal cells stop dividing when they touch neighbouring cells. A cell that loses it no longer recognises the stop signal and keeps dividing rapidly and uncontrollably. The abnormal cells accumulate into a mass — a tumour. Such tumours can be cancerous, invading nearby healthy tissues and spreading disease in the organism.

SA12. Draw a labelled diagram of a chloroplast. Briefly explain its role in a plant cell.

(Diagram: double membrane, stroma inside, stacks of disc-shaped membranes (grana) containing chlorophyll, ribosomes and DNA — see Topic 5 animation.)

The chloroplast is the plastid that contains the green pigment chlorophyll. Its role is photosynthesis: chlorophyll absorbs light energy, which is used with carbon dioxide and water to make sugars. These are stored temporarily as starch granules in the stroma and supply energy for the plant's growth.

SA13. Explain the structure and function of vacuoles in mature plant cells.

In a mature plant cell the vacuole is a large central sac bounded by a single selectively permeable membrane and filled with watery cell sap containing dissolved water, minerals, sugars and wastes. It may occupy most of the cell's volume. Functions: storage of nutrients, isolation of harmful wastes, and — because the fluid presses against the cell wall — maintaining the rigidity and firmness (turgor) of the plant.

SA14. What are plastids? How do chromoplasts differ from leucoplasts in content and function?

Plastids are double-membrane organelles found only in plant cells (and algae), responsible for food synthesis and storage; they have their own DNA and ribosomes. Chromoplasts contain yellow, orange or red pigments and give bright colours to flowers and fruits to attract pollinators and seed-dispersing animals. Leucoplasts are colourless (no pigment) and store food such as starch, oils or proteins.

SA15. A student states that mitochondria and plastids can synthesise some of their own proteins independently. Is this correct? Provide reasoning.

Yes. Both mitochondria and plastids are unique among organelles in possessing their own genetic material (circular DNA) and their own ribosomes. Ribosomes are the sites of protein synthesis, so these organelles do not have to depend entirely on the nucleus and cytoplasmic ribosomes — they can independently manufacture some of the specific proteins they need.

SA16. Compare Rough ER and Smooth ER based on microscopic appearance and primary functions.

RER looks bumpy/rough under the electron microscope because ribosomes stud its surface; it synthesises and secretes proteins such as digestive enzymes and hormones. SER lacks ribosomes, so it appears smooth; it synthesises and stores fats and lipids (needed for membranes) and helps detoxify harmful chemicals.

SA17. Identify the type of cell division that occurs in reproductive organs. Explain how it maintains the chromosome number across generations.

Meiosis. It is a two-step division producing four gametes (sperm/eggs, pollen/egg cells), each with half the chromosome number of the parent cell. At fertilisation a male and a female gamete fuse, and the two half-sets combine to restore the full, original chromosome number in the offspring — so the number stays constant generation after generation.

SA18. What is a selectively permeable membrane? Why is it important for cells rather than a fully permeable one?

A selectively permeable membrane allows only certain substances to pass and blocks others. It lets the cell control its internal environment: useful substances like oxygen, water and nutrients enter, while large or unwanted molecules are kept out, and wastes such as carbon dioxide diffuse out. If the membrane were fully permeable, toxins could flood in and essential molecules leak out, leading to cell death.

SA19. Trace and explain the path of a newly synthesised protein from its origin to its secretion outside the cell.

Ribosomes on the RER synthesise the protein → it is enclosed in a transport vesicle and sent to the Golgi apparatus → the Golgi modifies the protein and sorts it → it is packaged into a secretory vesicle → the vesicle travels through the cytoplasm, fuses with the plasma membrane and releases the protein outside the cell.

SA20. What is the primary function of mitochondria? What would happen to an animal cell if all its mitochondria were removed?

Mitochondria carry out cellular respiration — breaking down glucose to produce ATP, the cell's energy currency. Without mitochondria the cell would have no ATP to power chemical reactions, synthesise molecules or transport substances across the membrane. All vital processes would halt and the cell would quickly die.

SA21. Explain how technological interventions like the electron microscope facilitated new knowledge about cells.

Early light microscopes had limited magnification and resolution, showing only cell boundaries and large parts. The electron microscope uses a focused beam of electrons instead of light, allowing structures to be seen at the nanometre scale with great clarity. This revealed sub-cellular details previously hidden — ribosomes, mitochondria, ER, Golgi, membranes — and led to a deep understanding of organelles.

SA22. Draw a labelled diagram of a nucleus. What role does chromatin play when a cell is about to divide?

(Diagram: double-layered nuclear membrane with nuclear pores, nucleolus, chromatin network — see Topic 4 animation.)

In a non-dividing cell the genetic material is an entangled mass of thread-like chromatin containing DNA. When the cell prepares to divide, the chromatin condenses and coils tightly into distinct rod-shaped chromosomes. This organised form allows the genetic material to be easily and equally distributed to the daughter cells.

SA23. What is a nucleoid? How does the genetic material of a prokaryotic cell differ from that of a eukaryotic cell?

A nucleoid is the irregular, undefined region in a prokaryotic cell where the genetic material lies free in the cytoplasm without a membrane. Prokaryotic genetic material is a single circular DNA molecule. Eukaryotic genetic material is enclosed in a double-membraned nucleus and organised into multiple rod-shaped chromosomes (DNA with proteins), allowing far more complex instructions.

SA24. Draw the experimental setup to demonstrate osmosis using empty potato cups. Why is it necessary to keep one cup completely empty?

(Setup: four peeled potato cups in troughs of water — A empty, B with sugar, C with salt, D boiled potato with sugar.)

The empty cup (A) is the control. It shows that water does not gather in the hollow on its own, proving that the water collecting in the other cups is due only to the osmotically active solute (sugar or salt) creating a concentration gradient.

SA25. Describe Programmed Cell Death and explain its significance in the development of an organism.

Programmed Cell Death (PCD) is a genetically regulated, highly organised process of selective cell destruction. Cells execute it when they are severely damaged, dangerously mutated or no longer needed. It is essential for normal development, quality control and immune function. Example: in the early embryo, PCD removes the tissue between developing digits so that separate fingers and toes form instead of webbed hands.

SA26. Under which condition would a plant cell experience shrinkage of its inner contents away from the cell wall? Name this process.

When the plant cell is placed in a hypertonic (highly concentrated) solution. The outside solute concentration is higher than inside, so water rapidly exits by osmosis; the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink and pull away from the rigid cell wall. This process is called plasmolysis.

SA27. What is cell culture? Why is it necessary to maintain strict sterile conditions?

Cell culture is growing plant or animal cells outside the body in a nutrient-rich medium under controlled temperature, acidity and humidity, so they multiply. Strict sterility is critical because the nutrient medium is highly susceptible to contamination — invading bacteria or fungi would compete for nutrients and rapidly destroy the cultured cells, ruining the experiment or the production of medicines and vaccines.

SA28. Why does a carrot placed in plain water stay crunchy? How does this differ when placed in salt solution?

Plain water is hypotonic to carrot cells, so water enters by osmosis; the cells swell and press outward on their rigid walls, making the carrot stiff and crunchy (turgid). Concentrated salt solution is hypertonic; it draws water out of the cells, which lose turgor pressure and shrink, leaving the carrot limp and rubbery.

SA29. Your friend claims that removing the nucleus from a mature red blood cell increases its lifespan. Do you agree? Justify.

No. Mature RBCs naturally lack a nucleus so that more haemoglobin can be packed in for oxygen transport. But without a nucleus and its genetic instructions, the cell cannot make new proteins, repair damage or divide. Its lifespan is therefore drastically shortened — only about 120 days. Lacking a nucleus does not increase lifespan; it limits it.

SA30. Compare plant and animal cells with respect to their outer boundaries and the size of their vacuoles.

Outer boundary: an animal cell is enclosed only by a thin flexible cell membrane; a plant cell has, in addition, a rigid outer cell wall of cellulose giving structural support. Vacuoles: a mature plant cell has a single very large central vacuole occupying most of its volume (maintaining turgidity); animal cells have no vacuoles or only small, temporary ones.

D. Long Answer Questions

LA1. (a) Draw a labelled diagram of the fluid-mosaic model of the cell membrane. (b) How does this structure facilitate osmosis when a cell is placed in a hypotonic solution?

(a) (Diagram: lipid bilayer with heads outward and tails inward, proteins embedded across it, channel proteins — see the animation on Topic 2.)

(b) The bilayer lets small molecules like water pass but blocks most solutes, and channel proteins provide selective passages — so the membrane is selectively permeable. In a hypotonic solution the outside has a higher water concentration than the cell interior, so water moves into the cell through the membrane by osmosis; the cell swells and its volume increases (an animal cell may burst; a plant cell becomes turgid).

LA2. (a) Differentiate between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells regarding nuclear structure and membrane-bound organelles. (b) What is a nucleoid? (c) Why do mature RBCs lack a nucleus, and how does this affect their lifespan?

(a) Prokaryotic cells (bacteria) lack a well-defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotic cells have a prominent double-membrane nucleus containing chromosomes and many membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi, etc.).

(b) In prokaryotes the genetic material is not enclosed by a membrane; it is a single circular DNA in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid.

(c) Mature mammalian RBCs are enucleate — the absence of a nucleus makes room for more haemoglobin, greatly increasing oxygen-carrying capacity. But without genetic instructions they cannot repair themselves or divide, so they live only about 120 days.

LA3. (a) How do the RER and SER differ in microscopic appearance and primary functions? (b) Explain the functional relationship between the ER and the Golgi apparatus.

(a) RER appears rough because ribosomes are attached to its surface; it is the main site of protein synthesis and secretion. SER lacks ribosomes and appears smooth; it synthesises and stores fats and lipids.

(b) The ER and Golgi work in close coordination. Proteins and lipids made in the ER are sent in vesicles to the Golgi apparatus (stacks of flattened sacs — the packaging and dispatch centre). There they are modified, sorted and packaged into small vesicles for transport to destinations inside the cell (e.g., lysosomes) or outside it (secretion).

LA4. (a) Draw a well-labelled diagram of a mitochondrion. (b) Why is it called the powerhouse of the cell? (c) Mention two structural similarities between mitochondria and plastids that suggest a shared evolutionary history.

(a) (Diagram: outer membrane, inner membrane folded into cristae, matrix with DNA and ribosomes — see Topic 5.)

(b) Mitochondria generate the energy for almost all cellular activities: during cellular respiration glucose is broken down and the released energy is stored as ATP, the energy currency used for mechanical and chemical work.

(c) Both are enclosed by double membranes, and both contain their own circular DNA and ribosomes, enabling them to make some of their own proteins — features resembling free-living bacteria.

LA5. (a) Define cell division and state its significance. (b) How does mitosis differ from meiosis in the number of daughter cells and their chromosome number? (c) What would happen if a skin cell divided by meiosis instead of mitosis?

(a) Cell division is the process by which new cells are formed from pre-existing cells. It enables growth, repair of tissues and reproduction.

(b) Mitosis produces two genetically identical daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent (genetic stability). Meiosis, restricted to reproductive organs, is a two-step division producing four daughter cells each with half the chromosome number (genetic diversity).

(c) The skin cell would produce four cells with only half the genetic material; these would be non-functional, so wounds would not heal and skin integrity would be compromised.

LA6. (a) State the three main postulates of the classical Cell Theory. (b) What is contact inhibition in normal animal cells? (c) How does failure of contact inhibition lead to tumours?

(a) Formulated by Schleiden, Schwann and Virchow: (1) all living organisms are composed of one or more cells; (2) the cell is the basic unit of structure and function; (3) all new cells arise from pre-existing cells by cell division.

(b) Normal animal cells grow and divide in a controlled manner; when they come into physical contact with neighbouring cells, their division stops. This regulatory mechanism is contact inhibition, which maintains tissue balance.

(c) When cells undergo genetic changes and become cancerous, they lose contact inhibition, ignore the stop signals from surrounding cells and continue dividing uncontrollably, forming dangerous masses called tumours.

LA7. (a) Draw a structural diagram comparing a plant cell in pure water and in a concentrated sugar solution. (b) Explain the mechanical role of the cell wall in each solution.

(a) (Cell A in pure water: full, turgid, membrane pressed against wall, large central vacuole. Cell B in sugar solution: wall unchanged but cytoplasm shrunk away from wall — see Topic 3 animation.)

(b) The cell wall is a rigid outer covering of cellulose. In pure water (hypotonic) water enters by osmosis; the cell swells and fluid pressure builds against the wall, which exerts an equal and opposite pressure, preventing bursting and keeping the cell firm and turgid. In concentrated sugar solution (hypertonic) water leaves; the contents shrink and pull away from the wall (plasmolysis), but the wall stays firm and retains its shape, protecting the cell from complete collapse.

LA8. (a) Contrast the size and primary function of vacuoles in mature plant cells with those in animal cells. (b) Explain how lysosomes function as the waste-disposal system and keep the cell chemically clean.

(a) In mature plant cells vacuoles are extremely large, often occupying most of the cell volume, bounded by a selectively permeable membrane and filled with cell sap; they store water, sugars and minerals and provide turgidity to keep the plant firm and upright. Animal cells lack vacuoles or have very small ones that serve mainly for temporary storage.

(b) Lysosomes are membrane-bound sacs filled with powerful digestive enzymes. When waste materials and damaged organelles accumulate, lysosomes fuse with them and their enzymes break the complex structures into simpler substances, which are released into the cytoplasm for safe reuse — keeping the cell chemically clean.

LA9. (a) What are plastids and in which organisms are they found? (b) Categorise plastids based on pigments and mention the function of each. (c) Why do fruits and flowers exhibit bright colours in nature?

(a) Plastids are specialised double-membrane organelles found exclusively in plant cells and certain algae; they are centres of food synthesis and storage.

(b) Chloroplasts — contain green chlorophyll, capture solar energy for photosynthesis. Chromoplasts — contain yellow, orange or red pigments, colour flowers and fruits. Leucoplasts — colourless, store starch, oils or proteins (e.g., potato).

(c) The bright colours come from chromoplasts abundant in petals and fruit skins. They visually attract insects and birds for pollination and animals for eating fruits and dispersing seeds widely.

LA10. (a) Who discovered cells and how did technological improvements in microscopes deepen understanding of cell structures? (b) Briefly describe J. Craig Venter's 2010 experiment on synthetic biology. (c) Does this mean scientists can create life entirely from non-living materials?

(a) Robert Hooke discovered cells in 1665 in a thin slice of cork under a self-designed microscope. Over the centuries improvements in magnification, resolution and contrast — culminating in the electron microscope — let scientists see organelles at the nanometre scale.

(b) In 2010 Craig Venter's team chemically synthesised an exact copy of a bacterium's entire DNA and inserted it into another bacterial cell whose original DNA had been removed. The host cell grew and divided according to the synthetic instructions.

(c) No. Only the DNA was synthetic; the cytoplasm and cellular machinery came from a pre-existing living cell. So life was not created from scratch.

Her brother explains that mature plant cells have large central storage sacs filled with fluid whose pressure pushes against the cell boundaries, maintaining the plant's shape and firmness.

(a) Identify the large central storage sacs.

➜ Vacuoles (the central vacuole).

(b) What is the watery fluid inside these sacs called?

➜ Cell sap.

(c) Explain scientifically why the leaves drooped and then became firm again.

➜ Without water the vacuoles lost their stored water, internal pressure dropped, cells became less firm and the plant wilted. After watering, the vacuoles absorbed water by osmosis and swelled, restoring pressure against the cell walls and making the plant firm and upright.

Aryan scrapes his knee badly. A scab forms and within days the skin heals completely as new skin cells replace the damaged ones. Around the same time his mother gives birth to a baby girl. His grandmother remarks that the baby has eyes exactly like her father but hair like her mother. Aryan wonders how his skin copies itself perfectly to heal, while his sister is a mixture of traits from both parents.

(a) Which type of cell division heals Aryan's knee?

➜ Mitosis — responsible for growth, repair and replacement of damaged cells.

(b) How does the chromosome number of the new skin cells compare with the original?

➜ Exactly the same; the daughter cells are genetically identical to the parent cells.

(c) Name the type of cell division that produces the gametes in the parents.

➜ Meiosis, occurring in the reproductive organs (testes and ovaries).

(d) Why does the second type of division result in the baby sharing traits from both parents?

➜ Meiosis halves the chromosome number in each gamete. At fertilisation the sperm and egg fuse, so the baby receives half its genetic information from the mother and half from the father — a new combination rather than an identical copy.

Mr. Sharma discovers a small unusual lump on his arm. After a biopsy the doctor explains that normal body cells grow, divide and die in a highly regulated manner and naturally stop dividing when they touch neighbouring cells. In the lump, a few cells had lost this control, ignored the stop signals and continued dividing uncontrollably.

(a) What is the medical term for the mass of abnormal cells?

➜ A tumour.

(b) Name the phenomenon where normal cells stop dividing on contact with neighbours.

➜ Contact inhibition.

(c) According to the Cell Theory, where do all new cells in Mr. Sharma's body originate from?

➜ From pre-existing cells, through cell division.

(d) What is Programmed Cell Death and why is it essential?

➜ A genetically regulated process of selective cell destruction, essential for normal development, quality control, maintaining balance and immune function.

(e) Name the three scientists whose work led to the Cell Theory.

➜ Matthias Schleiden, Theodor Schwann and Rudolf Virchow.
