2. The Cell Membrane — Selective Permeability and Osmosis

Plasma membrane · potato experiment · diffusion vs osmosis · hypotonic / isotonic / hypertonic · fluid-mosaic model

2.2.1 Cell membrane — the universal feature of a cell

For cells to work as a team they must interact with each other and with their surroundings. These interactions happen at the cell boundary, where substances move in and out. Every cell — even a single-celled organism — has this boundary: the cell membrane, also called the plasma membrane. It is a very thin layer that surrounds the cell, protects its contents and gives the cell its individuality (it makes "this cell" distinct from "that cell").

The most important property of the membrane is that it is selectively permeable: it allows some substances to pass through while blocking others. Recall how oxygen and carbon dioxide cross the membranes of the alveoli in your lungs — that crossing is controlled by the membrane's structure.

Activity 2.2 — the potato experiment

Cut a potato into two pieces of roughly equal size. Weigh each piece and record the initial weight.
Put one piece in Beaker A — plain water, the other in Beaker B — 20% salt (or sugar) solution.
Leave undisturbed for about an hour. Weigh again.
Observation: Beaker A — the piece swells and its weight increases. Beaker B — the piece shrinks and its weight decreases.
Why? The cell membranes of the potato cells let water pass but not salt or sugar. Water always moves from where there is more water and less solute (dilute) to where there is less water and more solute (concentrated), until the two sides become equal. In Beaker A the water outside is purer than the cell sap → water enters the cells → the piece swells. In Beaker B the salt solution outside is more concentrated than the cell sap → water leaves the cells → the piece shrinks.
Why equal-sized pieces and initial weights? (Pause and Ponder Q3) To make a fair comparison. If the pieces were different sizes, a difference in final weight could be due to size, not to the liquid. Recording the initial weight lets us calculate the actual change for each piece.

Diffusion and osmosis

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration. It happens because particles are always moving and mixing. A difference in concentration between two places is called a concentration gradient. Diffusion needs no membrane — remember a drop of dye spreading in water, or fragrance spreading through a room.
Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane — water moves from the dilute side (more water) to the concentrated side (less water). Osmosis is how water from the soil enters plant root cells.

What happens to a cell in different solutions? (Fig. 2.6)

Solution outside the cellMeaningWater movementResult
Isotonicsolute outside = solute insideequal in both directionsno change in size
Hypotonicsolute outside < solute inside (e.g., pure water)net flow into the cellcell swells (animal cells may burst)
Hypertonicsolute outside > solute inside (e.g., 20% salt)net flow out of the cellcell shrinks
Memory tip: "Hypo" = below (less solute outside → water comes in → cell swells). "Hyper" = above (more solute outside → water goes out → cell shrinks). Always compare the solute concentration outside with that inside.

What if… mung bean seeds soaked in water for 12 hours are then put in a concentrated solution? The swollen seeds would lose water by osmosis and shrink, because the outside is now hypertonic.

Structure of the cell membrane — the fluid-mosaic model (Fig. 2.7)

The membrane is extremely thin — only 7 to 10 nanometres (1 nm = 0.000001 mm), so it can be seen only with an electron microscope. It is made of lipids (fats) and proteins. The fluid-mosaic model explains its structure:

  • Lipid bilayer: two layers of special fat molecules. Each molecule has a water-attracting head and water-repelling tails. Since there is water both outside the cell and inside (cytoplasm), the heads face outwards on both surfaces and the tails hide inwards, away from water. This oily middle layer is what stops most water-soluble substances (like salt) from simply passing through.
  • Proteins are embedded in the bilayer. Some form channels or carriers that act like gatekeepers, letting specific substances through — this is the basis of selective permeability.
  • Fluid: the molecules can move sideways, flip and rotate within the layer, so the membrane behaves like a fluid — flexible, self-sealing and able to change shape.
  • Mosaic: the proteins are scattered among the lipids like tiles in a mosaic — hence the name.
How does this structure control movement? Small molecules such as oxygen, carbon dioxide and water slip through the lipid layer easily; charged particles and larger molecules such as salt ions and sugar cannot, unless a specific protein gate lets them in. That is why, in the potato experiment, water crossed but salt did not.

Extra: Endosmosis, Exosmosis, Plasmolysis & Other Transport

Endosmosis — water moves into the cell (cell in a hypotonic solution). Exosmosis — water moves out of the cell (hypertonic solution). When a plant cell loses water by exosmosis, its protoplasm shrinks away from the cell wall — this is plasmolysis; put the cell back in water and it recovers (deplasmolysis). The shrinking of an animal cell in a hypertonic solution is called crenation.

Other ways substances cross the membrane: Facilitated transport — carrier proteins in the membrane help specific substances move down their gradient without energy. Active transport — the cell uses energy (ATP from mitochondria) to move a substance against its concentration gradient, e.g., root cells absorbing minerals from dilute soil water. Endocytosis — bulk intake by engulfing: phagocytosis ("cell eating", e.g., Amoeba engulfing food, WBCs engulfing bacteria) and pinocytosis ("cell drinking"). Exocytosis — vesicles fuse with the membrane to release material outside.

Two simple demonstrations of osmosis. (1) Put dry raisins in water: they swell (endosmosis); transfer swollen raisins to concentrated sugar solution: they shrink again (exosmosis). (2) Keep two raw eggs in dilute HCl for an hour — the calcium carbonate shell dissolves, leaving the egg wrapped only in its membrane. Place one egg in water and the other in concentrated salt solution: after an hour the first swells (endosmosis) and the second shrinks (exosmosis).

Q1. The cell membrane is described as selectively permeable because it:
A. blocks all substances
B. allows all substances freely
C. allows some substances to pass while blocking others
D. is made only of proteins
✔ Correct answer: C. allows some substances to pass while blocking others
Selective permeability means controlled passage — water and gases cross easily; salt and sugar are blocked unless a protein gate lets them.
Q2. A potato piece kept in 20% salt solution for an hour will:
A. swell and gain weight
B. shrink and lose weight
C. show no change
D. turn blue
✔ Correct answer: B. shrink and lose weight
The salt solution is hypertonic; water leaves the potato cells by osmosis, so the piece shrinks.
Q3. Osmosis is best defined as:
A. movement of salt through a membrane
B. diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane
C. spreading of dye in water
D. movement of proteins
✔ Correct answer: B. diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane
Osmosis is specifically the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane, from dilute to concentrated solution.
Q4. A cell placed in a hypotonic solution will:
A. shrink
B. swell
C. stay the same
D. lose salt
✔ Correct answer: B. swell
Hypotonic = less solute outside than inside → water moves in → the cell swells.
Q5. In the fluid-mosaic model, the 'mosaic' refers to:
A. water molecules
B. proteins scattered among lipids like tiles
C. the cell wall pattern
D. chromosomes
✔ Correct answer: B. proteins scattered among lipids like tiles
Proteins are embedded in the lipid bilayer in a scattered, tile-like pattern.
Q6. In the lipid bilayer, the water-repelling tails point:
A. outwards to the surroundings
B. inwards, away from water
C. towards the nucleus only
D. in random directions
✔ Correct answer: B. inwards, away from water
Heads (water-loving) face the watery outside and inside; tails (water-repelling) hide in the middle.
Q7. The thickness of the cell membrane is about:
A. 7–10 mm
B. 7–10 µm
C. 7–10 nm
D. 7–10 cm
✔ Correct answer: C. 7–10 nm
It is only 7–10 nanometres thick — visible only with an electron microscope.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between diffusion and osmosis?

Diffusion is the net movement of any particles from higher to lower concentration and does not need a membrane. Osmosis is a special case: the movement of water only, through a selectively permeable membrane, from a dilute solution to a concentrated one.

Why does salt not enter the potato cells but water does?

The middle of the membrane is an oily lipid layer. Small uncharged molecules like water can slip through, but salt exists as charged ions that cannot cross the oily layer without a specific protein channel. So only water moves, and it moves by osmosis.

What happens to a red blood cell in pure water?

Pure water is hypotonic to the cell. Water rushes in by osmosis, the cell swells, and because animal cells have no cell wall it may burst. In a concentrated salt solution it would shrink.

Why is the membrane called 'fluid'?

Its lipid and protein molecules are not fixed; they drift sideways, rotate and even flip within the layer. This lets the membrane bend, change shape, pinch off vesicles and seal itself after a puncture.

How do plant roots absorb water from soil?

Soil water is usually more dilute than the cell sap inside root cells. So water enters the root cells by osmosis through their selectively permeable membranes, and passes on from cell to cell towards the stem.
